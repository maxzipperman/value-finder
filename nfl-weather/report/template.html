A follow-up to a 2014 CMC senior thesis

Twelve more seasons of wind, cold & closing lines

The thesis found that wind and cold cut NFL passing, and that visitors from warm climates suffered most in freezing games. This rebuilds the data from scratch, re-runs the original models, tests them on every season since, and checks whether the betting market prices any of it.

What held up, what didn't

Part 1 · Replication and out-of-sample test

Same models, new seasons

The thesis sample was games from 2002–2013, scraped from Pro-Football-Reference. The same game-book weather now ships in nflverse, and team box scores rebuilt from play-by-play match the thesis summary table almost exactly (home passer rating ). The rebuilt data does show slightly less wind than the 2014 scrape, 6.4 vs 7.4 mph on average, so coefficients should agree in sign and size rather than to the decimal.

Each finding below is estimated three ways with the thesis's exact specification (log outcomes, franchise, opponent and season fixed effects, domes coded 72°F and calm): the published 2014 coefficient, the same regression on the rebuilt 2002–13 data, and the same regression on the games from 2014–2025 that the thesis never saw.

Wind is the durable result. In the thesis, 10 mph of wind cut passing yards by . On the rebuilt 2002–13 data it is , and on the 12 new seasons . Completion percentage still falls in the wind, and visitors from windy home stadiums are still the most affected, though the gaps between groups are within noise.

Temperature mostly faded. The pooled cold effect on passing yards is per 10°F in the new seasons, and the heat penalty is gone. The split that reversed is who feels the cold: in the thesis, visitors were about twice as sensitive as home teams. Since 2014 it runs the other way (visitors , home teams ).

Acclimation weakened but did not vanish. Warm-climate visitors in freezing games still complete fewer passes ( per 10°F colder than home), but the passing-yards effect, the thesis's strongest result, is now and not significant.

Part 2 · The 2014 model, audited

What the original model does, and what to fix

The main thesis regression, in words. Each row is one team's offense in one game:

log(passing yards) = offense baseline + opponent baseline + season baseline
                   + b₁·wind (mph) + b₂·wind-chill temperature (°F)
                   + b₃·freezing + b₄·hot (80°F+)
                   + home + home × each weather term + noise

How to read it. Because the outcome is logged, 100 × a coefficient is roughly the percent change per unit, so b₁ = −0.0068 means about −0.7% passing yards per mph, or −6.8% per 10 mph. The three baselines are fixed effects. Each offense is compared with itself, against a given opponent, in a given season, so a bad team playing in Buffalo doesn't look like a weather effect. Robust standard errors allow the noise to be bigger in some games than others. Tables 6 and 7 run the same model separately for home and away teams. Tables 8 and 9 give visitors a temperature differential, the game temperature minus their own city's temperature that week, to test acclimation.

Verified. Rebuilt independently, the model reproduces: home passer rating averages 85.55 against the thesis's 85.57, and 10 mph of wind costs of passing yards against the published −6.8%. The one definitional difference is that the thesis's total yards were gross passing plus rushing, without subtracting sack yardage.

Figure 10 starts from that exact specification on the thesis's own seasons and applies one fix at a time. The headline numbers barely move, so the original conclusions were right. What changes is how the numbers should be read and how certain they are.

  • The home effect was the real misread. With home × weather interactions in the model, the “Home” coefficient is the home advantage in a 0°F game with no wind. Rushing yards come out + as estimated, but + at 60°F and average wind once the terms are centered. The same goes for “Wind”: with a home × wind term, it is the visitors' wind effect, not the league's (step 1).
  • Significance was overstated a little. Clustering by game widens the wind interval by about . Every headline still clears the bar.
  • Domes as 72°F calm days and wind counted twice (directly and inside wind chill) both nudged the wind effect toward zero. Fixing them moves it back to about −7%.
  • The things the thesis could not fix itself matter more for other outcomes than for passing yards. Log models drop every zero (the INT-rate regression lost 37% of its games), straight-line wind hides that nothing happens below about 10 mph, and rush share mixes strategy with game script. Parts 3 and 4 use Poisson models, wind bins, and pass rate over expected instead.

Part 3 · Taking it further

What the thesis couldn't see

The extended model keeps the thesis's question but changes the machinery. Team quality gets its own effect for each team-season, on offense and defense, instead of one effect per franchise. A week-of-season effect, identified from dome games, keeps “December” from masquerading as “cold”. Domes get their own category instead of being coded as a 72°F calm day. Wind and air temperature enter as separate bins, with rain and snow from hourly ERA5 reanalysis added. Standard errors are clustered by game, and the outcomes include play-by-play measures that did not exist in 2014: EPA per dropback, completion rate over expected, air yards, and pass rate over expected. Sample: team-games, 1999–2025.

Wind works like a dose. Under 15 mph little happens. At 15–19 mph and again past 20 mph, teams throw shorter and less often (pass rate over expected points at 20+), completions get harder than their difficulty implies (CPOE ), and passing yards fall by about per team. Rain costs about as much passing efficiency as 15–19 mph wind. Snow hurts passing as much as rain, but points don't fall, partly because rushing picks up. The betting section returns to it.

Part 4 · Gambling implications

Does the market price the weather?

This question has been asked before. Borghesi (2008) found that heat, wind and rain lowered scoring more than 1984–2004 totals allowed for, and Paul (2017) found the same for wind and humidity. The test here is whether that gap survived another two decades of an increasingly sharp market. If the weather effects are real and the market already knows them, the closing total should move by about as much as scoring does. Figure 6 compares the two with the same fixed effects: how much a condition changes actual combined points, and how much it changes the closing total.

A strategy that doesn't peek

Closing-line value (CLV) is the scoreboard used below. The closing total is the last number before kickoff, after the market has absorbed the final forecast, injuries and sharp money. Betting under 44 when it closes 42.5 is +1.5 points of CLV. Win–loss records need thousands of bets to separate skill from luck. CLV doesn't depend on the final score, so it shows much sooner whether a signal is getting ahead of the market. On NFL totals, half a point is worth roughly 1–2.5 points of win probability, more near common final totals.

Bucket tables flatter whoever picks the buckets. The walk-forward test fits a weather-only model of P(under) on prior seasons, then bets the next season whenever the model clears 53%, refitting every year from 2006 on. It went (, ROI at −110, one-sided p = against break-even). Two sharper checks, borrowed from the sharp-markets project:

  • Closing-line value. Where an opening line exists, those under bets beat the closing number of the time, by points on average. All outdoor totals drift points over the same window. Beating the close is the steadier evidence that the signal carries information the opener lacks.
  • Model vs. the de-vigged price. Against closing over/under prices de-vigged two ways (proportional and Shin), the model's Brier score is vs. for the market, better by a hair. Betting only where the two disagree by 6+ points won . Both de-vig methods agree.

Would a fancier model help?

The same walk-forward setup also tests two richer models: a logistic model with interactions (wind × the posted total, wind × cold, wind × rain), and gradient-boosted trees on raw weather plus the posted total and spread. Scores are Brier skill against simply predicting the prior seasons' under rate.

Spreads, team totals, and the acclimation angle

Wind does not help underdogs against the spread. The acclimation effect from Part 3 does show up in sides, though. When a dome or warm-climate team visits a freezing game, the home team covered of games, and the visitor's implied team total went under . Cold-climate visitors show nothing ( home covers). It is the right shape, but the sample is small.

Forecasts, not hindsight

Every backtest above uses the weather observed at kickoff, which no bettor has. Open-Meteo archives what its models forecast 1–3 days out starting in 2024. For outdoor games since then, the day-before forecast of kickoff wind correlates with the game-book reading, and the three-day forecast . That is good enough to spot a windy game early, which is where the open-to-close move lives.

The same seasons also show where hindsight flatters the backtest. Rain nobody forecast went against the total, while games with rain in the day-before forecast went : the close had already priced it. Most of the historical rain edge is surprise rain, which can't be bet ahead of time. Wind looks different but is no comfort either: 15+ mph games went in 2024–26 on observed wind, so the last two seasons were flat whatever the inputs. Treat the observed-weather results as an upper bound.

What this adds up to: weather is a real but small input. The durable angle is betting totals down early in the week when a strong-wind forecast firms up, before the rest of the market does. Closing-line value is the scoreboard, not the win rate.

Method

How it's built

The 2014 thesis used a Beautiful Soup scraper against Pro-Football-Reference, which now blocks scripts. The new pipeline pulls the same game-book weather from nflverse, rebuilds every team box score from play-by-play, adds hourly ERA5 weather from Open-Meteo for each stadium, NOAA daily highs and lows for each home city, and opening and closing lines scraped from the Sportsbook Reviews archive. Every source is cached, so a weekly rerun downloads only new games.

ERA5 wind is a grid average, not the stadium reading, so it is put on the game-book scale with a fitted calibration (game-book = × ERA5, r = ). It is used only where the game book has no reading, and for forecasts.

Limits

  • Observed kickoff weather stands in for the forecast in every backtest before 2024.
  • Closing lines come from nflverse and the Sportsbook Reviews archive rather than one sharp book. They agree within half a point on 94% of totals.
  • Snow and heavy-wind samples are small: snow games and games with 20+ mph wind.
  • Stadium wind is one number per game. Wind direction relative to the field, and swirl inside bowls like the Meadowlands, are not modeled.
